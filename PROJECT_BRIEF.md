## 1. Client Situation

The client delivers large-scale online and blended higher-education programmes.

Student data is generated across multiple processes:

- course/module presentations;
- student registration;
- demographic and contextual information;
- assessment scheduling;
- assessment submissions and scores;
- virtual-learning-environment activity;
- final academic outcomes.

These datasets have historically been analysed separately.

The Student Success function monitors withdrawal. Academic teams focus on assessments and pass rates. Digital Learning teams monitor platform engagement. Senior management receives several reports but does not have a reliable end-to-end view of how student behaviour develops over time.

Leadership is particularly concerned that some students appear to disengage well before formally withdrawing or failing, but current reporting identifies many cases too late for meaningful intervention.

## 2. Business Problem

The organisation does not currently have a trusted analytical view connecting:

- who enrolled;
- when they registered;
- how they engaged with digital learning;
- how they performed in assessments;
- whether their behaviour changed over time;
- what final outcome they achieved.

The project team has been commissioned to develop an evidence-based analytical solution that improves understanding of student engagement, progression and academic outcomes.

The team must avoid treating correlation as causation. The purpose is to improve decision support and identify patterns that may warrant intervention or further research.

## 3. Project Objectives

### A. Establish a trusted analytical foundation
Profile the supplied tables, establish grain and relationships, and document significant data-quality limitations.

### B. Create a reusable student analytics model
Develop reproducible analytical structures connecting registration, student characteristics, assessment activity, virtual-learning engagement and outcomes.

### C. Define a management KPI framework
Create clear definitions for metrics such as engagement, participation, submission behaviour, withdrawal, completion and attainment.

### D. Understand disengagement and outcome patterns
Identify meaningful patterns associated with withdrawal, failure, pass and distinction outcomes.

### E. Explore early-warning potential
Assess whether observable behavioural indicators appear early enough to support targeted student-support activity.

### F. Produce decision-support outputs
Provide management with a stakeholder-facing analytical product and concise recommendations.

## 4. Key Business Questions

1. How do registration, withdrawal and final outcomes differ across modules and presentations?
2. What does student engagement look like over the lifecycle of a module?
3. When does engagement begin to decline for students who later withdraw or fail?
4. How do assessment submission timing and scores relate to final outcomes?
5. Which combinations of engagement and assessment behaviour warrant closer investigation?
6. Are there meaningful differences across student demographic or contextual groups?
7. What constitutes a defensible definition of an "engaged" or "disengaging" student?
8. Which indicators could be monitored weekly by the Student Success function?
9. How early could potential support needs reasonably be identified?
10. What ethical or analytical risks could arise from using these indicators operationally?

## 5. Expected Analytical Work

Teams are expected to:

- profile all supplied tables;
- map table relationships;
- establish the correct analytical grain;
- develop repeatable transformation logic;
- create time-aware engagement measures;
- investigate missing and unusual values;
- define management KPIs;
- conduct exploratory and diagnostic analysis;
- compare patterns across student groups and course presentations;
- document limitations and potential bias;
- build a management-facing analytical product;
- provide a clear technical handover.

## 6. Constraints

- the data is historical and anonymised;
- the project must not make decisions about identifiable real students;
- raw data must remain unchanged;
- analytical transformations must be reproducible;
- conclusions must be evidence-based;
- correlation must not be described as causation;
- protected or demographic characteristics must be handled responsibly;
- predictive work, if attempted, must include limitations and evaluation;
- teams must not represent the simulated client as a real Mettelo customer.

## 7. Success Criteria

The project will be considered successful where the team can demonstrate:

- correct understanding of source-table grain and relationships;
- reproducible data preparation;
- defensible KPI definitions;
- meaningful engagement and outcome analysis;
- responsible interpretation of student characteristics;
- clear and usable stakeholder reporting;
- documented limitations and risks;
- sufficient technical documentation for handover;
- clear evidence of individual team contributions.

## 8. Final Review

The team will present its solution to a Mettelo review panel acting as the client's stakeholder group.

The panel may challenge:

- metric definitions;
- data model choices;
- assumptions;
- handling of missing data;
- interpretation of engagement behaviour;
- treatment of demographic variables;
- predictive methodology;
- recommendations;
- limitations.
